In Brief

A Florida commercial loan is underwritten on the property or business cash flow, documented with a promissory note, loan agreement, mortgage or security agreement, and usually a personal guaranty, then closed after title, survey, lien, and entity due diligence.

Florida adds its own layer: documentary stamp tax ($0.35 per $100) and nonrecurring intangible tax (2 mills) on mortgage-secured debt, usury ceilings of 18% or 25% depending on loan size, a writing requirement for credit agreements, disclosure rules for smaller non-bank loans, and judicial foreclosure if the loan goes bad.

Most expensive problems in these deals are found in title and lien searches, not in the interest rate.

A commercial loan in Florida looks a lot like one in Ohio or Oregon until you get to the closing statement. Then the state-specific line items show up: stamp taxes that can run five figures on a mid-size mortgage, a title commitment with requirements nobody budgeted time for, and loan documents drafted around Florida rules on usury, guaranties, and foreclosure.

This guide walks through how the process actually runs, from picking a lender to what happens if the loan defaults. It is written for business owners, investors, and developers borrowing money, and for the private and regional lenders on the other side of the table. The numbers and statutes are current as of September 2026.

01

Who Lends to Florida Businesses, and What Each One Looks For

Commercial lending simply means credit extended to a business or for a business purpose rather than for personal or household use. Who you borrow from matters as much as how much you borrow, because each lender type prices risk differently and writes different documents.

Banks and credit unions
Where it fits: Stabilized real estate, established operating businesses, lines of credit. Trade-off: Best pricing, but the most paperwork, tighter covenants, and full-recourse guaranties are common.
SBA lenders (7(a) and 504)
Where it fits: Owner-occupied real estate, equipment, business acquisitions, working capital. Trade-off: Lower down payments and longer terms; slower approvals and SBA-specific eligibility rules.
Private credit and debt funds
Where it fits: Larger acquisitions, recapitalizations, transitional assets. Trade-off: Flexible structures and speed; higher pricing and heavier reporting.
Hard money and bridge lenders
Where it fits: Short-term, collateral-driven deals, fix-and-reposition projects. Trade-off: Close fast, but rates and points are high and usury limits still apply.
Online and non-bank business lenders
Where it fits: Smaller working-capital loans and receivables financing. Trade-off: Quick decisions; often the most expensive money, and Florida now requires cost disclosures on many of these deals.

Across all of them, underwriting keeps coming back to three numbers:

Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)

Net operating income divided by annual debt payments. Banks commonly want 1.20x to 1.25x or better, meaning the property or business earns 20% to 25% more than it needs to cover the loan.

Loan-to-value (LTV)

Conventional commercial real estate loans usually land between 65% and 80% of appraised value. SBA programs can go higher; the SBA 504 program typically requires as little as 10% down on owner-occupied property, with more required for startups or special-purpose buildings.

Guarantor strength

Most small and mid-size commercial loans require personal guaranties from the owners, so lenders look at the guarantors’ credit, liquidity, and net worth. Many banks want personal credit in the high 600s or better, though there is no single statutory cutoff.

02

Matching the Loan Structure to What You’re Financing

The purpose of the money drives the structure, the collateral, and the documents. Here is how the common Florida products line up.

Acquisition and permanent loans
Typical use: Buying or refinancing office, retail, industrial, or multifamily property. Main collateral: Recorded mortgage on the real estate plus assignment of leases and rents. Watch for: Prepayment penalties, tenant estoppels, stamp and intangible tax.
Construction loans
Typical use: Ground-up or major renovation projects. Main collateral: Mortgage plus assignment of plans, permits, and contracts. Watch for: Draw procedures, construction lien priority, completion guaranties.
Asset-based lending (term or revolving)
Typical use: Working capital for companies with receivables and inventory. Main collateral: UCC security interest in receivables, inventory, equipment. Watch for: Borrowing-base reporting and field exams.
Equipment loans and leases
Typical use: Machinery, vehicles, specialized technology. Main collateral: The equipment itself. Watch for: Whether a “lease” is really a secured loan; fixture filings.
Acquisition financing and LBOs
Typical use: Buying a company’s stock or assets. Main collateral: Target’s assets and cash flow, pledged equity. Watch for: Intercreditor terms with mezzanine or seller debt.
Syndicated loans and participations
Typical use: Deals too large for one lender. Main collateral: Shared collateral package held by an agent. Watch for: Voting rights, agent authority, participant remedies.

Larger transactions may also involve letters of credit, which back a borrower’s payment or performance obligations to a third party, and interest-rate swaps, which let a borrower on a floating rate lock in a fixed cost. Swaps come with their own ISDA documents and termination costs, which borrowers routinely underestimate when they refinance early.

03

From Term Sheet to Funding: How a Florida Commercial Loan Closes

Application and underwriting

The borrower delivers two to three years of financials and tax returns, a rent roll or operating statements for real estate, entity documents, and a personal financial statement for each guarantor.

Term sheet, then commitment letter

The term sheet is usually non-binding. The commitment letter is where the real conditions live: appraisal value, DSCR tests, reserves, guaranty scope, and closing deadlines. Read the conditions list before paying a commitment fee.

Due diligence

Lender’s counsel orders a title commitment, survey, zoning report, Phase I environmental assessment, and lien and litigation searches. Personal property collateral is searched through the Florida Secured Transaction Registry, where Florida UCC financing statements are filed; fixture filings are recorded in the county official records.

Promissory note, loan agreement, mortgage or security agreement, guaranties, environmental indemnity, assignment of leases and rents, and, for leased properties, tenant estoppels and subordination, non-disturbance and attornment agreements (SNDAs). Deals with more than one lender add intercreditor or subordination agreements.

Closing and post-closing

Documents are signed, the mortgage is recorded with stamp and intangible taxes paid, the lender’s title policy is issued, UCC-1 statements are filed, and funds are disbursed. Missing a post-closing filing can leave a lender unperfected, which matters a great deal if another creditor or a bankruptcy trustee shows up.

Timing varies with complexity. A small equipment loan or line of credit can close in one to three weeks. A commercial real estate loan more often takes 45 to 90 days, and the long pole is almost always third-party reports and title curative work, not the lender’s credit committee.

04

The Florida Rules That Change the Math

Documentary stamp tax and nonrecurring intangible tax

Under section 201.08, Florida Statutes, notes and other written obligations to pay money carry documentary stamp tax of $0.35 per $100 (or portion of $100). When the debt is secured by a recorded Florida mortgage, the tax is paid on the mortgage at recording and is based on the full amount secured, with no cap. Separately, section 199.133 imposes a one-time nonrecurring intangible tax of 2 mills ($2 per $1,000) on obligations secured by a mortgage or lien on Florida real property.

Two details trip people up. First, the Florida Department of Revenue caps stamp tax on an unsecured note at $2,450, a cap that disappears once the debt is secured by a recorded Florida mortgage. Second, certain renewal notes that add no new money are exempt under section 201.09, which is worth structuring around when you refinance or extend a loan with the same lender.

Here is how the taxes compare on a $1,000,000 loan:

Secured by a mortgage on Florida real estate
Stamp tax: $3,500. Intangible tax: $2,000.
Unsecured note signed in Florida
Stamp tax: $2,450 (capped). Intangible tax:
Secured only by equipment and receivables
Stamp tax: $2,450 (capped). Intangible tax:

On the mortgage-secured loan, the combined taxes total $5,500, or $5.50 per $1,000 borrowed. Revolving and future-advance facilities follow separate Department of Revenue rules on when tax is due, so the calculation is not always a straight multiplication.

Usury ceilings still apply to business loans

Florida’s usury limits are not limited to consumer loans. Under section 687.02 and section 687.03, the ceiling is 18% simple interest per year on loans of $500,000 or less. On loans above $500,000, a rate is not usurious unless it exceeds the criminal threshold in section 687.071, which makes willfully charging more than 25% a misdemeanor and more than 45% a felony, and can render the debt unenforceable.

The trap for private and hard money lenders is that points, certain fees, and other charges for the use of money can be counted as interest and spread over the loan term when testing the rate. A 13% note with heavy points on a short-term bridge loan can test well above its face rate. Lenders should run the effective-rate math before closing, and borrowers should ask for it.

Credit agreements must be in writing

Florida’s credit agreement statute of frauds, section 687.0304, bars a borrower from suing on a credit agreement unless it is in writing, states the terms, and is signed by the lender and borrower. In practice, a loan officer’s verbal promise to extend a maturity date or waive a default is not something a borrower can enforce. Get every modification, forbearance, and extension in a signed document.

Disclosure rules for smaller non-bank financing

Since January 1, 2024, the Florida Commercial Financing Disclosure Law (sections 559.961–559.9615) has required non-bank providers to give written cost disclosures, including the total amount financed, total repayment, and total dollar cost, on many commercial financing transactions of $500,000 or less. The law exempts banks and other depository institutions, loans secured by real property, leases, and providers who close five or fewer Florida transactions in a calendar year. It also restricts advance fees charged by commercial financing brokers.

Security interests in personal property

Florida’s version of UCC Article 9 is Chapter 679, Florida Statutes. A lender generally perfects its interest in equipment, inventory, and receivables by filing a UCC-1 with the Florida Secured Transaction Registry, and in fixtures by a fixture filing in the county where the real estate sits. Priority usually goes to whoever files first, which is why pre-closing searches matter so much.

05

The Fixture Filing Nobody Remembered: A Jacksonville Warehouse Refinance

A recent refinance handled by Lieberman Chamberlain PLLC shows why the search results matter more than the rate sheet. A family-owned distribution company on Jacksonville’s Westside was refinancing its warehouse with a regional bank to pull out equity for new racking and loading equipment. The term sheet was signed, the appraisal came in strong, and everyone expected a routine 60-day close.

The title and lien searches told a different story. An equipment lessor from years earlier still had a fixture filing of record against the property, covering conveyor systems the company believed it had paid off. There was also an unreleased notice of commencement from a roof replacement, which left open the possibility of construction lien claims. Either item would have made the bank’s mortgage less than first priority on part of the collateral, and the bank’s commitment letter required a clean first lien.

Andy Lieberman, drawing on his years as counsel to a title insurance underwriter, worked the problem from the title side. The firm tracked down the old lessor’s successor, obtained a payoff confirmation and a recorded termination of the fixture filing, and secured a contractor’s final affidavit and lien releases for the roof work so the title agent could delete the construction lien exception. The loan closed about two weeks later than planned, but the bank received the lien position it bargained for and the borrower avoided a reduced loan amount.

Client details have been changed to protect confidentiality. Every transaction turns on its own facts, and prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome.

06

Guaranties, Spouses, and Florida Homestead

For most closely held borrowers, the personal guaranty is the most consequential document in the stack. Three Florida-specific points deserve attention before signing:

  • Guaranties can be full, limited to a dollar amount, or limited to “bad boy” acts such as fraud or unauthorized transfers. The scope is negotiable at the term-sheet stage and almost never afterward.
  • Spousal signatures. Federal Regulation B generally prohibits a lender from requiring a creditworthy applicant’s spouse to sign a guaranty just because they are married (12 C.F.R. § 1002.7(d)). Lenders who ask for a spouse’s signature by default create risk for themselves.
  • Florida’s constitutional homestead protection (Article X, Section 4) shields a guarantor’s primary residence from most judgment creditors, subject to acreage limits and exceptions. It is one reason lenders ask for additional collateral from Florida guarantors, and one reason guarantors should not pledge their home casually.

07

When a Commercial Loan Defaults in Florida

Florida is a judicial foreclosure state. Under Chapter 702, a lender must file suit to foreclose a mortgage, and contested commercial foreclosures can take many months. Lenders sometimes use the expedited order-to-show-cause procedure in section 702.10 and often ask the court to appoint a receiver to collect rents while the case is pending.

Personal property collateral works differently. After default, a secured party may repossess equipment and other collateral without going to court as long as it does so without a breach of the peace (section 679.609), and must then dispose of it in a commercially reasonable manner.

Deadlines matter on both sides. Actions on a written contract and actions to foreclose a mortgage generally carry a five-year limitations period under section 95.11, and the one-year deficiency deadline in that statute applies only to certain owner-occupied residential property, not commercial loans. Borrowers facing default should talk to counsel before signing any forbearance agreement, because those agreements typically include releases and waivers that are hard to undo.

08

What Makes Florida Lending Different From Other States

Beyond the statutes, Florida deals carry market-specific risk. Property and windstorm insurance costs have become a real underwriting issue, and lenders now test DSCR with current premiums rather than last year’s. For buildings in a special flood hazard area, federally regulated lenders must require flood insurance under the National Flood Insurance Act, and much of coastal Florida sits in those zones.

Florida also has niche lending markets that barely exist elsewhere, such as financing secured by timeshare receivables and resort development loans. In Northeast Florida specifically, the growth of distribution and logistics space has changed how lenders underwrite industrial property, a trend covered in our look at evolving lending strategies in Jacksonville’s industrial boom. And because so much capital comes from out of state, many Florida loans are documented by lenders unfamiliar with Florida stamp tax, usury, and homestead rules, which is where expensive mistakes tend to happen.

09

Where Legal Review Actually Pays for Itself

Borrowers tend to focus on rate and leverage. The provisions that cause trouble later are usually elsewhere: prepayment and yield-maintenance terms, cross-default clauses tied to other loans, financial covenants tested quarterly, recourse carve-outs, and cash-management triggers. On the lender side, the risk is in collateral descriptions, perfection, title exceptions, and usury exposure on private deals.

A Florida commercial lending attorney who also understands title insurance can catch many of these issues before the commitment fee is paid, when there is still leverage to fix them. For lenders, the same review is what makes the documents enforceable if the loan ever has to be collected.

Frequently Asked Questions About Commercial Lending in Florida

Q.01What interest rates are Florida commercial borrowers seeing in 2026?

Most commercial loans are priced as a spread over an index. The prime rate rose to 7.00% on September 17, 2026, after the Federal Reserve raised its target range (see the Federal Reserve H.15 release for current benchmarks), so floating-rate bank loans are generally prime plus a margin. For SBA 7(a) loans over $350,000, the SBA caps the rate at the base rate plus 3%. Fixed-rate commercial mortgages track Treasury yields plus a spread, and hard money loans typically price well above bank debt. Rates move quickly, so treat any quoted figure as a snapshot.

Q.02How much is the documentary stamp tax on a Florida commercial mortgage?

It is $0.35 per $100 of the amount secured, plus nonrecurring intangible tax of $2 per $1,000. On a $1,000,000 mortgage, that is $3,500 plus $2,000, or $5,500 total. Unsecured notes are capped at $2,450 in stamp tax and owe no intangible tax.

Q.03What is the maximum legal interest rate on a commercial loan in Florida?

For loans of $500,000 or less, 18% per year simple interest. For loans over $500,000, the practical ceiling is 25%, the point at which criminal usury begins under section 687.071. Fees and points can count toward the effective rate.

Q.04How much down payment does a commercial real estate loan require?

Conventional loans usually require 20% to 35% equity. SBA 504 loans on owner-occupied property can go as low as 10% down, with higher contributions for startups and special-purpose properties such as hotels.

Q.05Can a business with weak credit get a commercial loan in Florida?

Often, yes, but at a price. Hard money and bridge lenders focus on collateral value, and some lenders underwrite primarily on the property’s cash flow. Expect higher rates, points, lower leverage, and shorter terms, and have the effective rate checked against Florida’s usury limits.

Q.06What happens if a borrower defaults on a Florida commercial loan?

The lender enforces the remedies in the loan documents: judicial foreclosure of real estate under Chapter 702, repossession and sale of personal property collateral under Chapter 679, and a lawsuit against guarantors for any shortfall. Many defaults are resolved through a negotiated forbearance or loan modification before litigation.

Q.07Do I need a lawyer for a commercial loan in Florida?

No statute requires one, but lenders almost always have counsel drafting the documents, and those documents are written to protect the lender. Borrowers who negotiate guaranty scope, prepayment terms, and default provisions before signing usually get meaningfully better terms than those who sign the first draft.
