What does a real estate closing attorney do in Jacksonville?

A title company insures title and runs the closing, but it can’t give you legal advice or argue your side. A lawyer can. Here’s where we come in:

Contract review

Ideally before you sign. We check the inspection period, financing and appraisal contingencies, deadlines, and who pays for what. Our buyer’s guide to contract contingencies covers this in more depth.

Title review

We read the title commitment and tell you which exceptions matter. Liens, judgments against a seller, and gaps in the chain of title need to be cleared before closing.

Curative work

If a problem turns up, we work on fixing it: payoff letters, lien releases, corrective deeds, probate or heirship paperwork, and, when nothing else works, a quiet title action.

Documents, closing, and recording

We review the deed, affidavits, settlement statement, and loan package, and check that everything meets Florida’s signing and recording rules.

02

How a home closing works in Duval County, step by step

1. Signed contract and escrow deposit

Most Jacksonville home sales use one of the standard Florida Realtors/Florida Bar contract forms. Once both sides sign, the deposit goes to the escrow agent and the deadlines start running. Missing an inspection or financing deadline can cost a buyer the deposit.

2. Title search and title commitment

The title agent searches the public records and issues a commitment. It lists what has to happen before a policy will issue and what the policy won’t cover. This is where most problems show up. Our page on title searches in Jacksonville explains what a search covers.

3. Survey, inspections, and HOA or condo estoppel

A survey can show a fence over the line or an easement under the pool. In an HOA or condominium, the association’s estoppel certificate states what dues and assessments are owed.

4. Loan documents and the Closing Disclosure

If you’re financing, federal rules require your lender to give you the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before you close. Compare it with your Loan Estimate and raise anything that looks wrong right away, because a late change can move your closing date.

5. Signing

Under section 689.01, Florida Statutes, a deed has to be signed in front of two witnesses, and it’s notarized as well. Witnesses and notaries can often appear by audio-video technology, and section 695.26 requires the signers’ and witnesses’ names and post-office addresses to be printed on the deed before the Clerk will record it.

6. Recording and disbursement

After signing, the deed and mortgage go to the Duval County Clerk of Court’s recording department, which keeps the county’s Official Records. Recording isn’t a formality. Under section 695.01, an unrecorded deed or mortgage isn’t good against later buyers or creditors who don’t know about it. Once documents are recorded or released for recording, the funds go out and the keys change hands.

03

Florida closing costs and taxes on your settlement statement

Here’s how the standard Florida charges work on a $400,000 purchase with a $320,000 loan:

Documentary stamp tax on the deed

70 cents per $100 of the price under section 201.02, Florida Statutes. On $400,000, that’s $2,800.

Documentary stamp tax on the note and mortgage

35 cents per $100 of the loan (section 201.08). On $320,000, that’s $1,120.

Intangible tax on the mortgage

2 mills, or $2 per $1,000 borrowed (section 199.133). On $320,000, that’s $640.

Everything else

recording fees, title insurance premiums, lender charges, prorated property taxes, and HOA or condo items.

Who pays each item depends on your contract. Under the standard forms commonly used in Northeast Florida, the seller usually pays the deed stamps and the buyer pays the loan-related taxes, but the parties can agree to something different

Frequently asked questions

Q.01Do I need a lawyer to close on a house in Florida?

No. Many Florida closings are run by title agents alone, but a title agent can’t give you legal advice or negotiate for you. People usually hire a closing attorney when they want someone working only for them, or when the deal has a wrinkle such as an estate sale, a title problem, or a for-sale-by-owner deal.

Q.02Who pays closing costs in Florida, the buyer or the seller?

Your contract decides. Under forms common in Northeast Florida, sellers usually pay deed doc stamps and the cost of clearing their title; buyers usually pay loan-related costs. All of it is negotiable.

Q.03Who chooses the title company in Florida?

The contract does. Standard Florida forms let the parties pick whether the buyer or seller designates the closing agent and pays for the owner’s title policy.

Q.04How long does a house closing take in Florida?

The contract sets the closing date. Financed purchases commonly take 30 to 45 days; cash deals can move faster. Title problems, appraisal issues, or a late Closing Disclosure can push the date back.

Q.05How much are doc stamps on a house in Jacksonville?

The deed stamps are 70 cents per $100 of the sale price, so a $350,000 sale carries $2,450. If there’s a mortgage, add 35 cents per $100 of the loan for note stamps, plus intangible tax of $2 per $1,000 borrowed.

Q.06Can I close on a Jacksonville home if I live out of state?

Usually, yes. Florida often lets witnesses and notaries appear by audio-video technology, or you can sign before a local notary and two witnesses and mail the originals back. Your lender may have its own rules.
